The Topography of Synthetic Sexual Violence: Establishing the Baseline

The 11,400 Metric and the Public Proliferation of Abuse
The most highly documented and analytically vital episode of public generative AI abuse occurred between late December 2025 and early January 2026. This followed the introduction of an image-editing feature powered by the Grok AI tool on the platform X (formerly Twitter).1 During an 11-day window spanning December 29, 2025, to January 8, 2026, the AI model was weaponized by users in what researchers characterized as a mass digital undressing spree.1 The sheer volume of this production is critical for several analytical reasons, primarily because it occurred on a mainstream, highly visible global social media platform rather than on the dark web or anonymous image boards.1
The Center for Countering Digital Hate (CCDH), utilizing an AI evaluation tool with a rigorous 95% F1 accuracy score, analyzed a random sample of 20,000 images out of a total population of 4.6 million images produced by the feature during the studied timeframe.1 The findings from this sample demonstrated the immediate, overwhelming, and highly concentrated weaponization of the technology. Extrapolating the sample data across the total volume revealed that 65% of the generated output contained sexualized imagery, resulting in an estimated 3 million sexualized images generated in just over a week.1
Within this dataset, approximately 0.5% of the sample consisted of CSAM-adjacent or child-sexualized images, translating to roughly 23,000 explicit images of minors synthesized rapidly by the user base.1 At the peak of this unrestricted access, the AI tool produced an estimated 190 sexualized images per minute, equating to a staggering 11,400 sexualized images per hour circulating on a public social media platform.1 The willingness of users to generate and share 11,400 sexualized images per hour in a semi-public sphere indicates a profound and deeply concerning normalization of digital sexual violence.1 It demonstrates that a significant demographic of users feels a high degree of impunity, entitlement, and social validation in synthetically violating the bodily autonomy of photorealistic real women and minors.1
The NCMEC Anomaly and the Necessity of Manual Verification
A critical component of this baseline estimation is the careful filtering of automated corporate reporting to prevent data conflation. In 2025, the National Center for Missing and Exploited Children (NCMEC) received an alarming 485,000 reports of AI-related CSAM via their CyberTipline, with 380,000 originating from a single corporate entity (Amazon).1 However, deep forensic analysis revealed that these reports were almost exclusively historical hash matches.1 These were instances where the corporate entity scanned pre-existing datasets used to train AI models and matched them to legacy, photographically captured CSAM, rather than instances of newly generated synthetic material.1
According to forensic evaluations, nearly 80% of all "Generative AI" CyberTipline reports in the first half of 2025 involved no new AI-generated CSAM at all.1 This anomaly proves that raw CyberTipline volume from Electronic Service Providers cannot be blindly trusted as a metric for novel synthetic abuse.1 Consequently, true scientific confirmation relies on manual qualitative analysis and rigorous global population surveys. One such survey, the Disrupting Harm study conducted by UNICEF, ECPAT, and INTERPOL, provided a devastating confirmation of the scale of abuse, finding that at least 1.2 million children disclosed having had their images manipulated into sexually explicit deepfakes in a single year.1
