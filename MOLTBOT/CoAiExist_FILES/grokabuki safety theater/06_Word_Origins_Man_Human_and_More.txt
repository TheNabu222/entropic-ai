### 1\. "Man" vs. "Human"

This is the central point, and it is **true**. The two words are not related and come from completely different ancient roots.

*   **Human** comes from the Latin word _humanus_ (meaning "of man" or "human"). This itself is believed to be derived from the Latin word _homo_ ("man"), which in turn traces back to a Proto-Indo-European (PIE) root, **`*dhghem-`**, meaning "earth." The concept is that a "human" is an "earthling" or "earthly being," as distinct from the gods in the sky.
    
*   **Man** comes from Old English _man_ or _mann_. This traces back to a Proto-Germanic root, **`*mann-`**, which comes from a _different_ PIE root, **`*man-`** (which also meant "man" or "person").
    

So, while they look and sound somewhat similar today, they have entirely separate histories.

### 2\. The Old English Words for People

The post is also correct about how Old English speakers referred to people.

*   **`Man` (or `Mann`) = "Person"** This was the default, gender-neutral word for "a human being" or "a person." This is precisely why we have inherited compound words like:
    
    *   **Mankind:** Literally "the kin of people" or "the human race."
        
    *   **Manslaughter:** The "slaying of a person," without the specific intent (premeditation) that defines "murder."
        
*   **`Wer` = "Male Person"** This was the specific word for a man or a male. The post is spot-on that its most famous survival is in **werewolf**, which literally means "man-wolf." This root, _wer_, is also related to the Latin word _vir_ (as in "virile" or "virtue").
    
*   **`Wif` = "Female Person"** This was the specific word for a woman or a female. This word, of course, survived and narrowed in meaning to become our modern **wife** (a married woman). The post is also correct about **midwife**, which comes from Old English _mid-wīf_, or "with-woman"—the person who is _with_ the mother during childbirth.
    

Over time, the specific word _wer_ faded away, and the generic word _man_ began to be used for _both_ "person" and, more specifically, "male person." This is how _man_ eventually took on its modern, primarily male-gendered meaning, which can cause confusion when looking at older texts or compound words.
