A-Imdugud was an early ruler of the First Dynasty of Ur, circa 2600 BCE, whose name and likely tomb were discovered in the Royal Cemetery at Ur. He is named after the Sumerian god Imdugud, a lion-headed eagle. 
A-Imdugud
Reign: A-Imdugud was an early king of the First Dynasty of Ur, but he is not mentioned in the Sumerian King List. His existence is known from an inscribed seal found in the Royal Cemetery at Ur.
Known as: He is also known by the name Aja-Anzu. 
The tombs of Ur
A-Imdugud is associated with tomb PG 1236, which is believed to be the earliest and most monumental structure in the Royal Cemetery. 
Discoveries from PG 1236
Inscribed seal: A seal found in the tomb bears the name A-Imdugud/Aja-Anzu. It shows a nude hero fighting lions, along with a war scene reminiscent of the famous Standard of Ur.
Royal scepter: The tomb also contained a reconstructed gold scepter and other royal artifacts, confirming the high status of its occupant. 
The Royal Cemetery at Ur
Excavated by Sir Leonard Woolley in the 1920s, the Royal Cemetery at Ur contained hundreds of burials, including the "royal" tombs of the Sumerian elite. These burials, dating to the Early Dynastic IIIa period (around 2600–2500 BCE), are known for their spectacular grave goods and, in some cases, evidence of human sacrifice. The finds are housed in the British Museum, the University of Pennsylvania Museum, and the Iraq Museum.

The exact distance is not precisely recorded in available public sources, but the royal tombs were clustered together in a specific section of the cemetery. 
Location within the Royal Cemetery
A-Imdugud's tomb (PG 1236): This monumental twin tomb is considered one of the largest and earliest royal structures found at the cemetery.
Puabi's tomb (PG 800): Puabi's lavishly furnished, unlooted tomb was also found within the same cemetery, nearby other "royal" burials. 
Archaeologist Sir Leonard Woolley found all 16 of the royal graves in the same general area, which had been built on top of a rubbish dump just outside the city walls. The tombs were cut down into the ground in roughly parallel rows, with private graves filling the space around them. This placement indicates that they were all part of the same funerary complex.

The banquet scene on the Gan-Ekiga(k) cylinder seal from Tomb PG 1236 depicts a typical Sumerian feasting scene, featuring figures seated and holding drinking cups, sometimes attended by servants. The scene reinforces the elite's social status through the communal act of feasting and the display of wealth and hierarchy. 
While a definitive, highly detailed description of this specific seal's scene is not widely published, it can be understood through comparison with similar, well-documented seals from the Royal Cemetery at Ur. 
Key features of the typical Early Dynastic banquet scene
Seated figures: The main participants of the banquet are shown seated, often holding conical cups. The seal's impression usually emphasizes their elaborate dress and hairstyle, which would have signified their gender and status.
Servants and attendants: Smaller, standing figures are typically depicted alongside the seated banqueters. These figures are shown waiting on the elites, offering food and drink, or holding fans. This highlights the social hierarchy between the served and the server.
Drinking vessels: A common motif is the use of long drinking tubes or straws, often extending to a large jar placed on the floor or a small table. This is believed to have been a method for drinking beer.
Associated with status: In Early Dynastic Ur, cylinder seals featuring banquet scenes were considered prestigious items and were frequently buried with high-status individuals. The inclusion of a cylinder seal with the name "Gan-Ekiga(k)" suggests that this individual held an important role in the royal household, perhaps serving the deceased King A-Imdugud.
General composition: Like many Sumerian cylinder seals, the scene is likely carved in one or more horizontal registers. The repeated nature of banquet scenes on many seals and other artifacts, such as the "Peace" panel of the Standard of Ur, suggests the feast was an important social, political, and potentially religious ceremony.

Additional items recovered from Tomb PG 1236
Besides the cylinder seals and gold scepter, archaeologists found other artifacts in the death pit of Tomb PG 1236, as well as indications of items that had been looted. 
Surviving objects include:
Gold and lapis lazuli beads: These would have been part of elaborate jewelry, similar to the adornments worn by Queen Puabi and her attendants in other Ur tombs.
Embossed gold leaves: The design on these leaves was pressed into thin sheets of gold foil.
Servant burials: The "death pit" contained the remains of human attendants, some with less elaborate jewelry, who were likely ritually sacrificed to accompany the main occupant.
Evidence of looted goods: Archaeologists found impressions and residue that indicated where other items had been, particularly in the central, looted chamber. Much of the precious material, including vessels of gold, silver, and alabaster, was stolen, but its former presence could be inferred. 
Possibility of DNA residue in looted tombs
Yes, it is possible for DNA residue to remain even if the body is gone, though it is highly degraded and difficult to recover. Recent advances in ancient DNA (aDNA) science have made it possible to analyze genetic material from very old and poorly preserved remains. 
Extraction from tooth and bone powder: Modern techniques can extract DNA from the smallest traces of biological material. For example, usable aDNA has been recovered from bone and tooth powder, even from ancient Egyptian mummies that have been handled by many people over centuries.
Challenges in Mesopotamian context: Retrieving usable aDNA from the Royal Cemetery of Ur would be especially challenging due to several factors:
Climate: The hot, humid conditions in southern Iraq are not ideal for DNA preservation.
Condition of remains: The bodies in the tomb chambers and death pits at Ur are extremely poorly preserved, often appearing as little more than discolored dust or impressions.
Extensive looting: The primary burial chamber of PG 1236 was thoroughly plundered in antiquity. While some retainer bodies remain, they are very delicate.
Proof-of-concept success: Despite the difficulties, scientists have successfully extracted ancient DNA from remains in regions with similarly challenging preservation conditions. The primary question for Tomb PG 1236 would be whether any of the servant skeletons, or even trace amounts of human material in the tomb's soil, would yield viable DNA. It would be a long shot, but technically possible.

It is possible, and even likely, that King A-Imdugud was a contemporary of Queen Puabi, but the exact relationship is uncertain. Both are from the Early Dynastic IIIa period (c. 2600–2500 BCE) and were buried in the Royal Cemetery at Ur. 
Evidence for contemporaneity
Shared time period: Archaeological analysis places the burials in the Royal Cemetery of Ur, including the tomb of A-Imdugud (PG 1236) and Queen Puabi (PG 800), within a relatively narrow window of time during the Early Dynastic IIIa period.
Royal Cemetery context: Both royal tombs were discovered in the same cemetery, part of a cluster of elite burials.
High status: The immense wealth and retainer sacrifices found in both tombs indicate that A-Imdugud and Puabi were high-status members of the ruling elite, likely part of the same royal dynasty or a closely related one.
Possible dynastic connections: The seal of A-Imdugud is stylistically similar to that of Mesannepada, another early king of Ur. Some scholars suggest A-Imdugud and Puabi could have been related to Mesannepada, either as predecessors, successors, or close relations. 
Ambiguities and ongoing research
Imprecise dates: While the general era of both burials is known, archaeologists do not have the precise dating to definitively state that the two lived and reigned at the exact same time.
Succession debate: The Sumerian King List omits A-Imdugud, Meskalamdug, and other rulers from the Royal Cemetery, making the order of succession unclear. This makes it difficult to definitively determine the precise relationship between the two figures.
A-Imdugud as predecessor: Some chronological reconstructions place A-Imdugud slightly earlier than Queen Puabi, potentially making him an early predecessor in the dynasty.

Mesannepada was the founder and first king of the First Dynasty of Ur, reigning in the mid-26th century BCE. His name means "youngling chosen by An". 
Key aspects of his reign
Historicity: Mesannepada is the first ruler of Ur mentioned in the Sumerian King List whose historical existence has been verified by contemporary archaeological finds.
Conquest of Uruk: The Sumerian King List records that Mesannepada took control of the kingship after defeating Lugal-kitun of Uruk.
King of Kish: Mesannepada also assumed the prestigious title of "King of Kish," a ceremonial claim used by many rulers to signify dominance over Sumer. A seal impression bearing this title was found in the Royal Cemetery at Ur.
Monumental construction: Inscriptions credit Mesannepada with significant building projects, including the construction of the temple of Nin-hursag at Tell el-Obeid. He and his son Meskiagnun are also mentioned in the Tummal Inscription as upkeepers of the main temple in Nippur.
Family: His father was Meskalamdug, another early ruler of Ur whose tomb yielded a famous golden helmet and is a key figure in the Royal Cemetery. One of Mesannepada's sons, A'annepada, succeeded him and continued his building projects.
Royal Cemetery: While no specific tomb is definitively assigned to Mesannepada, some scholars suggest either tomb PG 1232 or PG 1237, the "Great Death-Pit," might belong to him. 
Relationship to other rulers
Meskalamdug: Mesannepada is believed to be the son of Meskalamdug, as indicated by an inscription found on a bead in Mari.
Queen Puabi: Mesannepada was a near contemporary of Queen Puabi, whose tomb is one of the most famous burials in the Royal Cemetery.
Gilgamesh: He and his son Meskiagnun are named alongside the epic hero Gilgamesh of Uruk on the Tummal Inscription, suggesting a significant political role.

Gilgamesh's position on the Sumerian King List is not about being the "first," but about his significance as a transitional figure from the mythical past to a more grounded historical era. The list is a complex, semi-legendary document, and Gilgamesh's placement actually highlights his profound status, not that he was a "loser." 
Here's a breakdown of Gilgamesh's role on the Sumerian King List and why it doesn't make him a "loser":
A historical kernel within a mythical shell: According to the Sumerian King List, Gilgamesh was the fifth king of the First Dynasty of Uruk. He is listed after several kings who supposedly ruled for thousands of years. After Gilgamesh, the lengths of the reigns become much shorter and align more closely with historical reality.
The last of the mythical rulers: Gilgamesh is the last king on the list to have an impossibly long reign of 126 years, marking him as the end of the mythical pre-Flood era. His reign serves as a pivot point, separating the purely legendary kings from those with a stronger historical basis.
A historical figure, but also a legend: While Gilgamesh was likely a real king who ruled Uruk, the myths that grew up around him were far more influential. He became a foundational figure in Mesopotamian mythology, with stories celebrating his heroic deeds and quest for immortality. This literary fame is why later kings, like Shulgi of Ur, would invoke his name to legitimize their own rule.
Enduring legacy: Ultimately, Gilgamesh's "immortality" came not from a magical plant, but from his epic tale and the city walls of Uruk that he built. The fact that people thousands of years later—and even today—still talk about him shows that he was anything but a "loser."

The Tummal Inscription is a Sumerian text detailing the construction and renovation of the Tummal sanctuary in Nippur, a significant cult site for the gods Enlil and Ninlil. It records a series of restoration projects, attributing them to various kings over different periods. The chronicle was written much later, during the Old Babylonian period (around 1900–1600 BCE), based on older and now-lost records. 
What the Tummal Inscription says about Mesannepada
The inscription mentions Mesannepada and his son, Meskiagnun, documenting their involvement in the restoration of the Tummal temple after it fell into ruin for a second time. 
The text reads: 
"Meš-Ane-pada built the Bur-šušua in Enlil's temple. Meš-ki-aĝ-nuna, son of Meš-Ane-pada, made the Tummal flourish and brought Ninlil into the Tummal."
This passage is significant for several reasons:
Historicity: The Tummal Inscription mentions several rulers, including Gilgamesh, Mesannepada, and kings of the later Third Dynasty of Ur, who are also listed in the Sumerian King List (SKL). This corroborates Mesannepada's historical existence, as confirmed by archaeological finds.
Legitimacy and hegemony: By performing restoration work on the main temple of Enlil, the chief deity of the Sumerian pantheon, Mesannepada and his son demonstrated their pious dedication and asserted their political authority over all of Sumer. The city of Nippur, as the religious center, was crucial for any ruler seeking to claim hegemony.
Independent account: The inscription provides a separate, though later, source for Mesannepada's reign, confirming his status as a key builder and religious figure. 
Contextual significance of the Tummal Inscription
The inscription provides a narrative structure by describing the cycles of the sanctuary falling into ruin and being restored by subsequent rulers. However, its chronology differs from the Sumerian King List, which has led to some scholarly debate. The order of rulers in the Tummal text (Enmebaragesi, Mesannepada, Gilgamesh) does not match the generally accepted order from the SKL, highlighting the complex and sometimes conflicting nature of ancient historical sources.
