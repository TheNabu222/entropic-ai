Yes, the apostle Paul of Tarsus is the same Paul who is associated with Thecla. The connection comes from the apocryphal book The Acts of Paul and Thecla, a 2nd-century text that describes Thecla's conversion to Christianity after hearing Paul preach. 
However, it's important to understand this story within its historical and theological context.
The story of Thecla
The conversion: According to The Acts of Paul and Thecla, Thecla was a young virgin from Iconium (in modern-day Turkey) who heard Paul preaching about chastity and the resurrection from a window. Inspired by his words, she broke off her engagement to a local man named Thamyris.
Persecution and miracles: Her enraged family and former fiancé had Paul arrested and Thecla condemned to death. She survived being burned at the stake when a storm miraculously extinguished the fire. Later, she was again condemned to be devoured by wild beasts, but was protected by a lioness and other miraculous events. In a moment of divine inspiration, she baptized herself in a tank of water while in the arena.
A woman preacher: Following her ordeals, Paul commissioned Thecla to preach the Gospel. She then traveled widely, converted many people, and became an influential female figure in early Christianity, venerated as a saint. 
Apocryphal vs. canonical
The story of Thecla is not found in the canonical New Testament but in apocryphal literature written decades after Paul's death. 
Canonical texts: The books considered part of the biblical canon (like Paul's letters and the book of Acts) are the most reliable source for the life of the historical Paul. These texts do not mention Thecla.
Apocryphal texts: The Acts of Paul and Thecla is a religious romance written around 180 CE. Church father Tertullian reports that the author, a presbyter in Asia, was deposed from his position for committing fraud by writing in Paul's name. The text's portrayal of Thecla as an independent female preacher sometimes ran counter to the stricter views on women's roles found in other early Christian writings. 
Theological significance
While the story is not considered historically factual by most scholars, Thecla became a popular figure in the Eastern Church. Her legend demonstrates the veneration of early Christian martyrs and the role of women in early Christianity. Her story also served as an inspiration for many ascetic and celibate women who followed her example.

is it peter or paul that complains about mary magdelne
Peter is the apostle who is depicted complaining about Mary Magdalene in several non-canonical, or Gnostic, texts. The apostle Paul is not mentioned in these accounts. The tensions between Peter and Mary Magdalene in these texts highlight an early Christian debate over leadership and the roles of women. 
Accounts of Peter complaining about Mary Magdalene
The Gospel of Mary: In this 2nd-century Coptic text, Mary Magdalene shares secret revelations she received from Jesus. Peter expresses skepticism and jealousy, questioning why Jesus would give special teachings to a woman privately and not openly to the male apostles. Levi (also called Matthew) defends Mary, telling Peter he has always been "hot-tempered".
The Gospel of Thomas: In the final saying (Saying 114) of this Gnostic gospel, Peter tells Jesus that Mary Magdalene should leave them, "for women are not worthy of the life". Jesus responds that he will guide her to become male so that she, too, can enter the Kingdom of Heaven.
Pistis Sophia: In this Gnostic text, Peter complains to Jesus that Mary Magdalene is dominating the conversation and taking priority away from the other apostles. Jesus rebukes Peter for his impatience.

Meaning of the conflict
Scholars interpret the conflict between Peter and Mary Magdalene in these Gnostic texts as a reflection of power struggles within the early Christian community. 
Authority and gender: Peter represents the traditional, patriarchal authority of the church, based on his status as the "rock" on which the church was built. Mary Magdalene, in these accounts, represents a competing tradition that elevated the spiritual authority of women and valued personal, mystical revelation.
Competing traditions: These texts can be seen as an attempt to legitimize or challenge different factions within early Christianity. The Gnostic texts that elevate Mary Magdalene's spiritual insight are countered by more mainstream Christian writings that emphasize male leadership.

how does paul respond
The apostle Paul does not respond to Peter's complaints about Mary Magdalene in the Gnostic texts because he is not present in those scenes. The dispute over Mary's spiritual authority is primarily a conflict between Peter and Mary Magdalene, with the apostle Levi (Matthew) sometimes acting as a mediator. 
Instead of Paul, it is Levi who steps in to defend Mary when Peter challenges her in the Gospel of Mary. 
Paul's absence from the Gnostic debate
The canonical letters of Paul were written in the mid-first century CE, decades before the Gnostic gospels that portray a conflict between Peter and Mary Magdalene. Paul's own writings do not mention Mary Magdalene at all. His understanding of authority in the early church is distinct from the mystical, revelatory authority Mary represents in the Gnostic texts. 
Instead, the primary confrontation between Paul and Peter recorded in scripture occurs in the canonical book of Galatians, where Paul publicly rebukes Peter for his hypocrisy on the issue of table fellowship with Gentiles. This was a very different kind of dispute, concerning the practical application of the gospel rather than spiritual revelations. 
How Levi defends Mary in the Gospel of Mary
In the Gospel of Mary, Mary has shared a secret vision she received from Jesus. When Peter expresses skepticism and jealousy, Levi intervenes: 
Rebukes Peter: Levi calls Peter "hot-tempered" and questions his right to challenge a woman whom Jesus has made worthy.
Validates Mary's testimony: He reminds the apostles that Jesus "loved her more than us" and that they should be ashamed of contending against her.
Urges obedience: Levi encourages the group to focus on Jesus' teachings rather than their internal divisions. He tells them to "preach the gospel, not laying down any other rule or other law beyond what the Savior said".

what was pauls ideas about women in the chuurch
Paul's ideas about women in the church are a subject of ongoing debate among scholars and theologians, as his writings contain statements that appear to both affirm women's roles and place restrictions on them. There is no single, straightforward summary, and the interpretation often depends on which passages are given greater emphasis, as well as their historical and cultural context. 
Passages affirming women's ministry and equality
Equal in Christ (Galatians 3:28): In a central statement of Christian egalitarianism, Paul famously declares, "There is neither Jew nor Gentile, neither slave nor free, nor is there male and female, for you are all one in Christ Jesus". Many see this as the foundational principle for understanding women's full and equal status in the church.
Female co-workers and leaders (Romans 16): In the final chapter of his letter to the Romans, Paul offers greetings and praise to several women who played prominent roles in the early church.
Phoebe: He refers to her as a "deacon" (diakonos) and "benefactor" (prostatis) of the church in Cenchreae, suggesting a position of leadership and prominence.
Priscilla: With her husband Aquila, she is named as a co-worker who helped instruct the male leader Apollos more accurately.
Junia: Paul refers to her as "outstanding among the apostles." While later translations changed her name to the male form "Junias," the early church fathers, who spoke Greek, understood Junia to be a female apostle.
Women praying and prophesying (1 Corinthians 11:5): Paul acknowledges that women pray and prophesy in church assemblies, which are important public ministries. The issue he addresses is not whether they can speak, but whether they are doing so in a culturally appropriate manner (with their heads covered). 
Passages seemingly restricting women's ministry
Women must be silent (1 Corinthians 14:34–35): In this passage, Paul appears to command women to be silent during church gatherings.
Contextual interpretation: Many scholars argue this was a local instruction addressing a specific problem in the Corinthian church, where uneducated wives may have been disrupting the service by loudly questioning their husbands.
Textual criticism: Some scholars believe this passage is a later interpolation, meaning it was not originally part of Paul's letter but added later, as it contradicts Paul's allowance for women to pray and prophesy earlier in the same letter.
"I do not permit a woman to teach or to assume authority over a man" (1 Timothy 2:11–15): This is one of the most debated texts concerning women's roles.
Authorship debate: The Pastoral Epistles (1 Timothy, 2 Timothy, and Titus) are widely believed by scholars to be pseudoepigraphal (not written by Paul) and were possibly composed later, in a more institutionalized and patriarchal period of the church.
Specific situation: Even if considered Pauline, many argue the passage addresses a specific, local problem with false teaching in Ephesus. It is argued that Paul's mention of Eve's deception and Adam being created first was intended to correct a specific false teaching influenced by the local Artemis cult, which promoted female superiority.
Headship language (1 Corinthians 11:3, Ephesians 5:22–24): Paul refers to the husband as the "head" of the wife. Interpretations differ on whether kephalē ("head") in this context means "authority over" or "source". Egalitarians often point to Genesis, where Eve was created from Adam, suggesting "source" is the intended meaning. 
The modern scholarly consensus
Many modern biblical scholars recognize the tension in the Pauline texts. Many view Paul's comments as situational, with his affirmations of women's ministry being the more accurate reflection of his theology and practice. His instructions on female submission are often understood as contextual responses to specific problems in different communities, or as a later, non-Pauline addition.

Phoebe: A deacon and benefactor
In Romans 16:1–2, Paul introduces Phoebe as a deacon (diakonos) of the church in Cenchreae and a benefactor (prostatis) to many, including himself. Her contributions included: 
Carrying the letter to the Romans: Paul entrusted Phoebe to be the official carrier of his letter to the church in Rome. As the letter's bearer, she was likely responsible for reading it aloud to the house churches and explaining its theological content.
A ministry leader and patron: Her designation as a prostatis (benefactor or patron) indicates she was a person of financial means and authority who used her resources to support the ministry of others, including Paul.
Serving as a deacon: Paul's use of the term diakonos indicates that Phoebe held an official, recognized ministry position within her church. 
Priscilla: A co-worker and teacher
Priscilla and her husband Aquila were a missionary couple and key allies of Paul. Notably, Priscilla's name is mentioned first in several passages, which is unusual for the time and may indicate her prominence in their shared ministry. 
Co-laborers with Paul: Priscilla and Aquila were tentmakers who worked alongside Paul and accompanied him on his missionary journeys.
A powerful teacher: The book of Acts records how Priscilla and Aquila mentored the eloquent preacher Apollos, "explaining to him the way of God more accurately" (Acts 18:26). This shows Priscilla's deep knowledge of Christian teaching and her role as a respected theological mentor to a prominent male leader.
Hosts of house churches: The couple hosted church gatherings in their home in different cities, including Ephesus and Rome, providing a vital meeting place for early Christian communities.
Risking their lives: In Romans 16:3–4, Paul notes that Priscilla and Aquila "risked their necks" for his life, a gesture for which he and all the Gentile churches were grateful. 
Junia: An outstanding apostle
Junia is mentioned once in the New Testament (Romans 16:7). She is described alongside Andronicus as "outstanding among the apostles". 
An apostle and a fellow prisoner: The Greek word for "apostle" was not limited to the original twelve disciples. Paul's reference, along with the fact that she was imprisoned with him, indicates Junia was a respected missionary and church planter.
A leader from before Paul: The text notes that Junia was "in Christ before I was" (Romans 16:7). This suggests she was an early convert to Christianity and a veteran missionary when Paul wrote his letter.
Long-recognized as a female apostle: Early church fathers, including John Chrysostom, acknowledged Junia as a woman and praised her high standing. The controversy over her gender and role was a later development, with modern scholarship affirming her female identity.

What other roles did women play in the early church besides Phoebe, Priscilla, and Junia?
In addition to the notable figures of Phoebe, Priscilla, and Junia, women in the early church served in various crucial roles as missionaries, patrons, prophets, and disciples. Their contributions highlight that women were not simply passive participants but active agents in the spread and development of early Christianity. 
Patrons and financial supporters
Many women used their financial resources to support Jesus and the apostles, enabling the ministry to expand.
Mary Magdalene, Joanna, and Susanna: Luke 8:1–3 explicitly names these women as disciples who traveled with Jesus and the Twelve and "provided for them out of their means". Joanna was the wife of Chuza, an official in Herod's court, suggesting a high social status.
Lydia: This wealthy merchant from Thyatira was the first documented convert in Europe after hearing Paul's message in Philippi. Her entire household was baptized, and she hosted Paul and his companions in her home, establishing the first European church there.
Mary, mother of John Mark: Her home in Jerusalem was a gathering place for believers, as noted in Acts 12:12. 
Prophets
The gift of prophecy was not limited to men in the early church.
The four daughters of Philip: Philip the Evangelist had four unmarried daughters who were known as prophetesses. The book of Acts mentions that Paul and his companions stayed with them in Caesarea. Church historian Eusebius later wrote that these women were "great lights" of the church in Asia, sought out for their wisdom.
Prophetess Anna: As described in Luke's Gospel, Anna was a prophetess who spent her life worshipping at the temple. When Jesus was presented as an infant, she recognized his significance and "spoke about the child to all who were waiting for the redemption of Jerusalem". 
Leaders of house churches
As Christianity spread, meetings often took place in private homes, many of which were owned and led by women.
Nympha: In Colossians 4:15, Paul sends greetings to Nympha and "the church in her house". Hosting a church in a home was a significant position of leadership in the early Christian movement. 
Charitable workers and evangelists
Beyond formal ministry roles, many women performed vital acts of charity and evangelism.
Tabitha (Dorcas): This disciple from Joppa was "always doing good and helping the poor" by making clothes for widows. Her resurrection by Peter, recorded in Acts 9, highlights her importance in the community.
The Samaritan woman: Although unnamed, her conversation with Jesus at the well is a powerful example of female evangelism. She became a missionary to her own community, inviting her townspeople to hear Jesus' message. 
Disciples and witnesses
Women were among Jesus' first and most dedicated disciples, and were often the first witnesses to his most pivotal moments.
Mary and Martha of Bethany: These sisters were close friends of Jesus, hosting him and learning at his feet. Mary, in particular, is noted for choosing to sit and learn from Jesus, a rare and radical position for a woman at the time.
Mary Magdalene: She was the first to witness Jesus' resurrection and was commissioned to tell the male disciples, earning her the traditional title "Apostle to the Apostles".
