Connections with Greek and Latin roots
Cloaca and cluere: The Latin noun cloaca ("sewer") comes from the Latin verb cluere ("to purify" or "to cleanse"). This forms a direct etymological link.
Cloacina: As a cult-title for Venus, Cloacina means "The Purifier". This confirms her association with cleansing and sanitation, connecting her to the sewer system, the Cloaca Maxima.
κλύζειν (klúzein): The Greek verb klúzein ("to wash out" or "to cleanse") is cognate with the Latin cluere, further establishing the shared Indo-European root for the concept of cleansing.
luere (i.e. purgare): This is a separate, though semantically related, link. The Latin verb luere ("to wash") also connects to the concept of purification, though its etymology is distinct from cluere. Its connection in the quoted passage serves to underscore the theme of ritual cleansing. 
The Sabine War and Venus Cluacina
The tradition that T. Tatius and Romulus, after the war following the Rape of the Sabine Women, ordered a purification ritual relates to the reconciliation between the two peoples. 
Purification ritual: The peace was marked by a cleansing ritual using myrtle, a plant sacred to Venus, near the shrine of Cloacina.
Union and reconciliation: The deity Cloacina was subsequently identified with Venus as Venus Cloacina, acknowledging her as a goddess of union, peace, and reconciliation, in addition to her role as "the Cleanser". The ritual and the merger of the deities symbolized the political and social merging of the Romans and Sabines. 
The family names Cluilia and Cloelia
The names Cluilia and Cloelia are not directly derived from the same root as cluere ("to cleanse"), and their inclusion in the quoted passage appears to be an error or a speculative, likely incorrect, ancient association.
The name Cloelia is associated with a myth in which a Roman girl held hostage by Lars Porsena escapes and swims across the Tiber River. This has no connection to the etymological root of cloaca.
The name Cluilius is associated with the Roman gens Cluilia, which had Etruscan and Latin origins, but there is no reliable scholarly evidence connecting its etymology to the root of cloaca or cluere. 
The connection proposed in the statement between Cloelia and Cluilia and the term cloaca and its etymological roots is spurious. However, the connection between cloaca, cluere, κλύζειν, and the role of Venus Cluacina in the purification ritual following the Sabine War is well-supported by historical and etymological sources. 

The word cloaca is connected with cluere, Cluilia, Cloelia, and κλύζειν through shared linguistic roots related to washing, cleansing, and renown. The Latin verb luere is linked as a synonym for purgare (to purge), aligning with the concept of cleansing. 
Cloaca and cleansing verbs
The word cloaca comes from the Latin verb cluere ("to cleanse"). 
Cloaca: This Latin word means "public sewer" or "drain," referring to a place for cleansing or washing away waste. The famous Roman sewer system was called the Cloaca Maxima.
κλύζειν (klýzein): This is a Greek verb meaning "to wash off" or "to rinse out". It shares an ancient Proto-Indo-European (PIE) root, *kleu-, with the Latin cluere.
luere: This Latin verb means "to wash." In the context of etymology, it is associated with the idea of purification (purgare), which connects it to the cleansing theme of cluere and cloaca. 
Cloelia and Cluilia: Renown and fame
The names Cloelia and Cluilia are derived from a different, though related, sense of the Latin verb cluere, meaning "to be renowned" or "famous". 
Cloelia: In Roman legend, Cloelia was a hostage who escaped from the Etruscans by swimming across the Tiber River. Her bravery made her a renowned figure.
Cluilia: This was a Roman family name, likely associated with the same concept of being "illustrious" or having great renown. 
Thematic connection
While the linguistic roots diverge slightly in their specific meaning ("to cleanse" vs. "to be renowned"), the original connections within Latin likely drew from a similar source. In Roman tradition, this overlap was highlighted by the story of Venus Cloacina ("Venus of the Sewer"), who was worshiped at the site of the Cloaca Maxima. The goddess was associated with purification, and the site became a place where peace treaties were ratified. This tradition demonstrates how the Roman mind could link the concepts of cleansing and fame or purification and renown, thus connecting the disparate words. 


Venus is the Goddess of love and beauty in Roman myth, though She likely had Her origins in an Italic Goddess of gardens, both of the ornamental and vegetable types. Her name comes from the neuter Latin venus meaning love, desire, beauty, or charm, which is related to the Latin verb venerari meaning to adore, honor, worship, or revere; the English word ‘venerate’ is also from that root. She was in time identified with the Greek Goddess Aphrodite, taking on Her mythology; and the Romans adopted the story of Her son, Aeneas, as a foundational myth of the city and empire of Rome.

In the Iliad, Aeneas is a heroic Trojan prince and the son of Aphrodite; though in various tales he meets various ends (generally peaceful) the Romans seized on the idea that after the war he set out for Italy with the remaining Trojan people to found Rome. Accordingly Aphrodite (Venus) became the divine ancestress of Rome, and indeed the Julian family (starting with Julius Caesar) claimed direct descent from Her. As was habit with the Romans, they used this divine link as propaganda to build up Rome and the Empire, in effect claiming its policy of conquest and domination as destiny.

As the divine guardian of Rome, Venus was given epithets of a political nature, implying Her approval; She was also conflated with several minor Goddesses such as Cloacina and Murcia. Here then are the various aspects and epithets of Venus.
