a lively affair 1912
A Lively Affair was a 1912 silent comedy film that served as anti-suffrage propaganda. The film used humor and a reversal of traditional gender roles to mock and discredit the women's suffrage movement, framing it as a threat to social and family order.

Synopsis
The plot of the film features a role reversal, with women behaving in a "mannish" way and men being "emasculated". 
Women leave their domestic duties and children behind to attend what their husbands believe is a serious suffrage meeting.
The meeting quickly devolves into a card game of poker and then a physical brawl among the women.
When police arrive to break up the fight, the women are arrested.
Meanwhile, their husbands, who have been forced to take care of the children and household, celebrate the punishment their wives receive. 
Historical context and intent
The film's satirical humor was intended to marginalize and trivialize the suffragette movement.
By portraying the women as irresponsible, masculine, and prone to fighting, the film argues that the suffragettes' demands for voting rights were a path to the destruction of the family and social order.
The film is often cited as an example of how early filmmakers used the new medium of cinema to comment on social and political issues.
It is part of the DVD compilation Treasures III: Social Issues in American Film, 1900–1934, which explores early cinema's engagement with various social causes. 
Production information
Production company: The film's production company is not definitively known, but evidence suggests it may have been Warner Bros. or Selig Co.
Availability: While parts of the film were lost, a restored version is available and included in the Treasures III DVD set.

Your reading of A Lively Affair highlights a common modern interpretation of the film, where the intended anti-suffrage message is subverted by how it portrays men. Instead of the men appearing competent and righteous, their domestic failures make their earlier positions seem hypocritical or even absurd. 
Here is an analysis of this "reverse effect":
The incompetence of the men
The gender role reversal: The plot depends on a role reversal, with the women attending a "meeting" and the men being left in charge of the household and children. The men immediately prove incompetent at domestic tasks and childcare, highlighting their reliance on their wives' labor.
A contrast with reality: During the suffrage era, men frequently argued that women's primary role was in the home and that they lacked the capacity for public life. The film's depiction of helpless husbands suggests that it is, in fact, the men who are incapable of managing the full responsibilities of domestic life. This makes the men's original objection to suffrage seem less like a genuine concern for order and more like a selfish desire to maintain their comfortable routine. 
The women's "masculine" behavior
Challenging the stereotype: The film intends to demonize the suffragettes by showing them behaving in "masculine" ways—playing poker and fighting. However, a modern viewer might see this not as an attack on women, but as a criticism of the aggressive, vice-filled male social sphere that the women are imitating.
A display of freedom: The women's "unruly" behavior can also be re-read as a liberating break from the suffocating expectations of their domestic lives. Their defiance, even in the form of a brawl, is a raw expression of the new freedoms they are exploring, not an indictment of their character. 
The male punishment and celebration
Cruel delight: The men celebrate when the women are arrested and punished. This reaction, meant to appear justified within the film, can now be seen as petty and cruel. It reveals a desire to control women and punish their aspirations for independence, rather than a genuine concern for family values.
No moral high ground: Because the men's behavior has already been shown to be absurd and ineffective, their moralizing at the end of the film rings hollow. A modern audience is more likely to question the husbands' self-righteousness than the wives' rebellion. 
This reinterpretation of A Lively Affair showcases how satire can backfire over time, especially as cultural values shift. What was once seen as a straightforward comedic attack on a progressive movement can, with a century of social change, be re-read as an accidental and powerful critique of the very establishment it was meant to defend.

How did the film's intended audience react in 1912?
Historians conclude that the anti-suffrage message of A Lively Affair likely resonated with its intended male audience in 1912, reinforcing their biases and anxieties about changing gender roles. However, the film's portrayal of men's incompetence was probably seen as harmless, even charming, humor rather than a significant criticism. Meanwhile, suffrage activists were not laughing, and the film helped galvanize the pro-suffrage movement to produce their own media. 
Response from the intended audience
For the majority of men in the early 20th century, anti-suffrage messaging, whether in cartoons or film, appealed to the anxieties they felt about changes to traditional gender roles and the family unit. 
The joke worked: For many male viewers, the comedy of A Lively Affair came from seeing women behave "mannishly" and abandon their domestic duties, confirming the stereotype of the suffrage activist as an irresponsible shrew.
A "charming" ending: The film's conclusion, where the men celebrate the women's arrest, would have been received by many as the proper restoration of social order. A modern viewer sees this as cruel, but in 1912, it was framed as a just punishment for stepping out of line.
A harmless critique: The film's joke about men being incompetent with childcare was likely viewed as lighthearted. It didn't threaten the established social structure but instead played on stereotypes for easy laughs. 
Response from the suffrage movement
Suffragists and their supporters were not amused by anti-suffrage films like A Lively Affair and saw them for what they were: propaganda designed to marginalize and trivialize their cause. 
Propaganda backfired: The proliferation of negative depictions of suffragettes in the press and on film was a key reason suffrage activists started producing their own media.
Spurred their own films: Following the release of films like A Lively Affair, two major women's rights groups began making their own narrative films and newsreels to counter the negative portrayals. One pro-suffrage film released in 1912 was Votes for Women.
Shifted the conversation: The negative films inadvertently helped elevate the suffrage movement's status. As film historian Shelly Stamp notes, the movement was "the earliest advocacy body" to utilize cinema for political commentary. The movement's leaders recognized the power of the new medium to reach the masses and decided to use it for their own purposes. 
Ultimately, while the film likely succeeded in getting laughs from its intended anti-suffrage audience, its effect was limited. It was one of many similar films and, by its very existence, helped spur the pro-suffrage movement to harness the power of cinema for its own message.

It is highly unlikely that any men in 1912 spoke up with the modern, subversive interpretation of A Lively Affair. Male supporters of suffrage certainly reacted strongly to anti-suffrage media, but they focused on defending the integrity of the women they supported rather than analyzing the film's unintended portrayal of masculinity. For most people, both critics and supporters, the film's message was unambiguous: women's suffrage would lead to a breakdown of traditional gender roles, with disastrous consequences. 
The prevailing interpretation
In 1912, the vast majority of media commentary—and thus the common public understanding—focused on the film's intended message. 
A "charming" caricature: The anti-suffrage message would likely have been taken at face value by most viewers, who would have found the men's incompetence harmlessly charming rather than an indictment of their masculinity. The humor was based on the "disaster" of men being forced to do women's work, not on questioning men's entitlement to a life free of domestic chores.
The real emasculation: From the perspective of anti-suffrage men, the true "emasculation" was the idea of women entering the public sphere, which was seen as a male domain. Suffrage propaganda consistently played on the fear that women gaining power would mean men losing it. 
Male suffrage supporters' focus
The men who supported women's suffrage in 1912 were a distinct minority who faced ridicule and condemnation for challenging gender norms. Their public statements and reactions focused on different aspects of the debate. 
The ridicule of male suffragists: Men who marched in suffrage parades were often heckled with taunts like "Who's taking care of the baby?" and "Look at the longhaired Susan". The men's response was typically to defend their position, not to deconstruct the anti-suffrage argument by pointing out its flaws.
Defending women's reputation: The "suffragents," as male supporters were called, were busy defending the intelligence and moral uprightness of suffragist women, who were routinely depicted as "mannish," ugly, and "shrews" in cartoons and films. Their focus was on countering the primary attack, not engaging with a subtle, unintended satirical effect.
Direct action and propaganda: Male suffrage organizations like the Men's League for Women's Suffrage countered anti-suffrage films by helping produce their own pro-suffrage films, such as Votes for Women (1912). Their goal was to produce more positive and direct propaganda, not to provide a critical analysis of anti-suffrage media. 
Why a modern interpretation was unlikely
A modern reading of A Lively Affair relies on a cultural and historical distance that did not exist in 1912.
Different comedic sensibility: The film's humor was not meant to be read ironically or satirically in the way a contemporary audience might.
Power dynamics: In 1912, men held nearly all of the political and economic power. The concept that their domestic failures in a farce could be seen as an indictment of their overall behavior would have been a non-starter.

What other unintended critiques of masculinity appeared in anti-suffrage propaganda?
Just as with A Lively Affair, other anti-suffrage propaganda featured unintentional critiques of masculinity by framing traditional gender roles as rigid, burdensome, or even absurd. While intending to warn against social chaos, the propaganda often highlighted the fragility of male power and the men's dependence on women's unpaid domestic labor. 
The fragile domestic patriarch
A central theme in anti-suffrage propaganda was the fear of gender inversion, where men and women would switch roles. Postcards and cartoons often showed overwhelmed men struggling with domestic duties while their wives were out voting or attending meetings. 
The "man baby": Some propaganda featured illustrations of men in aprons holding crying babies. The men's struggle to perform domestic tasks was meant to be a humorous warning of a dystopian future. However, this image now highlights the irony that the men who claimed to have a monopoly on public responsibility were entirely dependent on women for basic domestic labor.
A zero-sum game: As anti-suffrage scholar Maria Popova notes, these images worked from a "zero-sum mentality": if women gained rights, men lost them. The men's identity was defined in opposition to women. This dependence on women's subordination for their own sense of self comes across as fragile and weak to a modern observer.
The domestic sphere as a burden: The very act of portraying domestic work as a punishment for men inadvertently reinforces the idea that it was undervalued and undesirable labor. The propaganda suggests that a man forced into the domestic sphere was being "abused" and denied his freedom, which reveals that men did not perceive "women's work" as being in any way desirable. 
The hollow nature of male power
Some anti-suffrage propaganda also unintentionally revealed the performative and superficial nature of male authority in the absence of genuine partnership and respect for women's agency.
The "henpecked" husband: In some depictions, the formerly authoritative male figure is shown as timid or ineffectual, cowering behind his wife. This was meant to mock suffrage's effect on the man's power, but it also reveals how precarious and easily subverted that power could be.
Power over, not power with: The underlying message of much anti-suffrage material is that if men could not have power over women, they had no power at all. This interpretation casts men as controlling figures rather than loving partners. The men's ultimate "punishment" of celebrating their wives' arrest reveals a desire to maintain control through cruelty rather than through mutual respect. 
A warning to men, not just women
The propaganda's warnings about gender inversion were not just directed at women. They implicitly warned men against relinquishing their power, no matter how insignificant it might seem, because it would ultimately lead to their own "feminization". 
In this way, anti-suffrage propaganda can be reinterpreted today as a testament to the fact that traditional masculinity was often a demanding and rigid performance, predicated on maintaining a restrictive and unfair status quo.

can you point me to male authored suffragist pamphlets?
Numerous male suffragists authored pamphlets and essays to publicly advocate for women's voting rights, often publishing with or through suffrage organizations like the Men's League for Women's Suffrage. The following is a list of publications and authors that contributed to the suffrage cause: 
Early 19th-century abolitionists
Male abolitionists were often early and fervent supporters of women's rights, and their work provides some of the earliest male-authored suffrage arguments. 
Frederick Douglass: As a central figure in both the abolitionist and suffrage movements, Douglass penned multiple speeches and essays defending women's right to vote.
"The Rights of Women" (1848): Published in his newspaper, The North Star, this piece argues that "Right is of no sex".
"Woman Suffrage Movement" (1870): In this document, Douglass argues that since women owe allegiance to the government, they are "justly entitled to all we claim for man".
Parker Pillsbury: This American minister and advocate for both abolition and women's rights regularly contributed to suffrage publications. 
American Men's League for Women's Suffrage
This organization, founded in 1909, published a range of materials to encourage men to join the cause.
Max Eastman: The first secretary of the Men's League for Woman Suffrage of the State of New York, Eastman wrote essays and pamphlets to recruit men to the cause. His 1912 article, "Early History of the Men's League," detailed the group's formation.
Judge Benjamin Barr Lindsey: A Colorado judge who championed women's rights after his state passed women's suffrage, Lindsey published a pamphlet promoting the cause and highlighting the benefits of suffrage for both men and women.
Francis Minor: A lawyer and husband of suffragist Virginia Minor, he wrote a pamphlet in 1869 arguing that the Fourteenth Amendment had already made national women's suffrage legal. 
British men's suffrage organizations
British organizations, including the Men's League for Women's Suffrage (1907) and the Men's Political Union for Women's Enfranchisement (1910), also published supporting materials. 
Victor Duval: The founder of the Men's Political Union for Women's Enfranchisement, Duval published a pamphlet in 1910 titled An Appeal to Men.
John Stuart Mill: The influential 19th-century British philosopher and a Member of Parliament, Mill published his famous essay The Subjection of Women in 1869, which powerfully argued for the equality of the sexes. 
Collaborative efforts
Many pamphlets and publications were a mix of male and female voices or were published by female-led organizations but featured content from male supporters.
"A full pocket book of what great men and great women have thought of woman suffrage" (c. 1900–1910): Published by the New York Woman Suffrage Association, this pamphlet featured quotes in support of women's suffrage from prominent figures like Theodore Roosevelt, William H. Taft, and Frederick Douglass.
Common Sense Applied to Woman Suffrage (1894): While authored by Mary Putnam-Jacobi, this book draws upon Thomas Paine's famous pamphlet and features arguments from both men and women.

The suffragist vs. suffragette distinction
The key difference in terminology is critical to your search.

This included organizations in both the U.S. and the UK. Male supporters frequently authored or contributed to publications for these groups.
Suffragette: Used specifically for the militant British activists of the WSPU who used civil disobedience and property damage to draw attention to their cause. The WSPU's internal publications, like their newspaper The Suffragette, were written and edited by women.

Based on historical sources, numerous pamphlets, articles, and essays supporting women's suffrage were authored by men. These works were essential for persuading other men to support the cause. 
American authors and publications
Max Eastman: As the first secretary of the Men's League for Woman Suffrage of the State of New York, Eastman wrote essays and pamphlets to support the cause. His 1912 article, "Early History of the Men's League," detailed the group's formation. An archived version of his 1911 pamphlet, Is Woman Suffrage Important?, is available through Johns Hopkins University.
Frederick Douglass: The abolitionist and orator supported women's voting rights through his speeches and writings. A pamphlet from his newspaper, The North Star, featured his remarks from the Seneca Falls Convention in 1848.
Thomas Paine: Though predating the suffrage movement, Paine published "An Occasional Letter on the Female Sex" in 1775, arguing against the oppression of women. The essay, published anonymously, is considered an early work supporting women's rights.
Francis Minor: Minor, a lawyer and women's rights advocate, wrote a pamphlet in 1869. It argued that the Fourteenth Amendment had already granted women the right to vote. He used this argument when his wife was denied the right to vote, leading to the Supreme Court case Minor v. Happersett.
Archibald Grimké: As a lawyer, journalist, and NAACP founding member, Grimké published the pamphlet Why Disenfranchisement Is Bad in 1904. Though focused on voting rights for African Americans, it supported equal voting rights and was distributed with Booker T. Washington's help.
Judge Benjamin Barr Lindsey: Colorado judge and women's rights advocate Lindsey published the pamphlet If I Were a Woman. In it, he used his professional experience to argue for the benefits of women's suffrage. 
British authors and publications
John Stuart Mill: The 19th-century British philosopher is a foundational figure in male support for suffrage. His 1869 essay, The Subjection of Women, argues for the equality of the sexes, including women's right to vote.
Henry Nevinson: As a journalist and member of the Men's League for Women's Suffrage, Nevinson wrote pamphlets supporting the cause, including one condemning the force-feeding of a male hunger striker in 1912. 
Collections and resources

Brooke Kroeger's The Suffragents: This book provides an account of the men who worked for women's suffrage, particularly focusing on the Men's League for Woman Suffrage. It details how the movement used prominent men, such as Upton Sinclair and Max Eastman, to advance the cause.

What arguments did men use to persuade other men to support women's suffrage?
Male suffragists used a variety of arguments to persuade other men to support women's right to vote, ranging from appeals to justice to pragmatic arguments about politics. To succeed, they had to counter the prevailing beliefs that politics was a corrupting influence for women and that a man's vote already represented his entire household. 
Arguments grounded in democracy and justice
These arguments framed women's suffrage as a logical and necessary extension of existing democratic principles.
Natural rights: Drawing from Enlightenment ideals, suffragist men argued that those who are governed by laws should have a say in creating them. Frederick Douglass, for example, argued that if a government is "just which governs by the free consent of the governed," then women must be allowed to vote.
"No taxation without representation": This was a powerful callback to the American Revolution. Since women paid taxes, owned property, and worked, male suffragists contended it was unjust to deny them political representation.
Enlarging the electorate: Some male suffragists argued that extending the vote to women would improve the quality of the democratic process, not just its quantity. They believed that by increasing civic engagement and adding a new perspective, women would strengthen and refine the nation's political life. 
Pragmatic and political arguments
These arguments addressed common anxieties and attempted to show men that women's suffrage would benefit them directly.
A "cleaner" political sphere: Male supporters often suggested that women's participation would have a purifying effect on a political landscape widely perceived as corrupt and dirty. They argued that women would bring their "moral superiority" and domestic concerns to public issues, resulting in more humane and moral legislation.
Legislative and social reform: Some men argued that women's votes were necessary to pass specific progressive legislation. Many male temperance advocates, for instance, believed that giving women the vote would help ban alcohol, which they saw as a major cause of domestic abuse.
Winning the West: In American territories and states, particularly in the West, some men voted for suffrage for pragmatic reasons. In less populated areas, they saw it as a way to boost the electorate and attract more female settlers.
Political strategy during WWI: In the final push for the 19th Amendment, some congressmen were persuaded to back the federal amendment as a "war measure." They believed President Woodrow Wilson's democratic rhetoric and women's patriotic war work made it politically necessary to grant suffrage. 
Appealing to existing gender norms
Some male suffragists used existing gender norms to their advantage, assuring men that women's traditional roles would not be erased by the vote.
Domestic concerns in the public sphere: Instead of framing suffrage as women leaving the home, some arguments suggested it was an extension of their domestic duties. Male suffragists explained that the vote would allow women to better protect their homes and families by influencing public policy on issues such as sanitation, education, and child labor.
Preserving "exalted" womanhood: To counter the idea that women's participation would diminish their "special privileges," some male supporters argued that voting would empower women to advocate for their own protection and "elevation".
Respecting wives and mothers: Some men argued that supporting suffrage was a show of respect for the intelligence and character of their own wives and mothers. This framed supporting the cause as a matter of personal honor rather than political conviction.

Give examples of how male suffragists used existing gender norms in their arguments
Male suffragists often framed their arguments by appealing to and extending existing gender norms, rather than challenging them directly. This strategy made the idea of women's suffrage more palatable to a male audience concerned about disruptions to the traditional social order. They argued that giving women the vote would not upend their domestic role but rather give them the political tools to better perform their duties as wives and mothers. 
Using domesticity to argue for public involvement
Instead of portraying voting as women entering a radically new public sphere, male suffragists presented it as an extension of women's existing, valued domestic duties.
The "municipal housekeeper": This argument framed the city or state as a larger "house" that needed to be cleaned up. Male suffragists argued that just as a woman was responsible for the cleanliness and sanitation of her own home, her vote would allow her to influence public policy on sanitation, clean water, and garbage disposal. Pamphlets and speeches asked, "If we hold women responsible for the results [of unsanitary conditions], must we not, in simple justice, let them have something to say as to what these conditions shall be?".
Protecting children and family: This was a powerful emotional appeal. Male supporters argued that a woman's vote would be used to advocate for issues directly affecting the welfare of her children, such as child labor laws, education funding, and access to healthcare. This framing reassured men that women would use their vote responsibly, prioritizing family and not abandoning their core domestic duties. 
Appealing to women's moral superiority
Another common gender norm was the belief that women were more moral and less prone to corruption than men. Male suffragists used this to argue that women's votes would have a purifying effect on politics. 
Cleaning up politics: Many suffragists, male and female, argued that the political world was full of corruption, vice, and alcohol. They claimed women's participation would elevate the moral standards of government and lead to a "cleaner" political sphere.
The temperance movement: Male suffragists with ties to the temperance movement, such as Daniel Anthony, argued that giving women the vote would lead to the prohibition of alcohol. They believed this would reduce public drunkenness and domestic abuse, thereby protecting the sanctity of the home. 
Framing suffrage as a duty, not a right
To further make the case palatable, male suffragists sometimes presented voting not as a radical "right" that women were demanding, but as a moral "duty" they would assume.
Responsibility, not privilege: This argument suggested that women, because of their elevated moral and social standing, had a responsibility to participate in civic life to improve society. This was a subtle but effective way of reframing the vote as a serious, civic-minded responsibility rather than a frivolous or self-interested demand.

can you give me a broader history of men supporting womens rights throught history
While male support for women's rights has been historically limited and often conditional, a number of men have played crucial roles as advocates and allies. Their involvement spans centuries and has evolved alongside feminist movements. 
Enlightenment thinkers (17th–18th centuries)
Rationalist philosophy: French Enlightenment writers like François Poullain de La Barre and the Marquis de Condorcet argued for gender equality based on universal human rights.
Political critique: In his Persian Letters, Montesquieu used female characters to critique patriarchal systems, advancing arguments against despotism in both the public and private spheres.
Utilitarianism: English philosopher Jeremy Bentham advocated for equal rights for women in all areas, believing that any arbitrary denial of rights was unjust.
Thomas Paine: An American revolutionary and political philosopher, Paine argued against the oppression of women in his 1775 essay "An Occasional Letter on the Female Sex". 
Abolitionism and the first wave of feminism (19th century)
In the U.S. and Britain, the anti-slavery movement served as a training ground and catalyst for the women's rights movement. 
Frederick Douglass: The prominent abolitionist and former slave was a lifelong and outspoken supporter of women's rights. He famously attended and spoke at the Seneca Falls Convention in 1848, defending the radical idea of women's suffrage when it was controversial even among activists.
William Lloyd Garrison: At the 1840 World Anti-Slavery Convention in London, Garrison and other male abolitionists sat silently with the women who had been excluded from participating, demonstrating solidarity.
John Stuart Mill: The British philosopher and Member of Parliament published the foundational feminist text The Subjection of Women (1869). Mill presented a petition for women's suffrage to Parliament in 1866 and continued to campaign for equal rights.
Henry Blackwell: A dedicated activist, Blackwell married suffragist Lucy Stone and publicly issued a protest against the unjust marriage laws of the era. 
Organized male support for suffrage (early 20th century)
As the suffrage movement gained momentum, men formed their own leagues to support the cause, primarily in the U.S. and Britain. 
Men's League for Women's Suffrage (U.S.): Founded in 1908, this organization included prominent figures like socialists Upton Sinclair and Max Eastman. The league's members wrote pamphlets, spoke at rallies, and marched in parades to lend political and moral support.
Men's Political Union for Women's Enfranchisement (UK): This more militant British organization was a male counterpart to the Women's Social and Political Union (WSPU). Members like George Lansbury and Henry Nevinson were arrested and imprisoned for supporting the cause. 
Second-wave feminism and beyond (mid-20th century to present)
In the 1960s and 1970s, male support for feminism was often met with suspicion, but some men continued to be active.
Advocating for the ERA: Actors like Alan Alda advocated for the Equal Rights Amendment during the 1970s and 1980s.
Academic support: Men have engaged in pro-feminist scholarship, challenging traditional masculine norms and advocating for gender equality in academic and social circles.
Modern allies: In recent years, men have been encouraged to become allies in the fight for gender equity through campaigns like the UN Women's HeForShe, which highlights the benefits of equality for men as well.

What are some criticisms of male involvement in historical feminist movements?
Men's involvement in feminist movements has been a complex and often criticized aspect of feminist history. While some men have acted as sincere and effective allies, critics argue that the involvement of men, especially those in positions of power, has sometimes undermined the very movements they claimed to support. 
Various feminist perspectives have raised criticisms regarding the involvement of men in historical feminist movements. These criticisms often highlight potential challenges and complexities that can arise when men participate in movements primarily focused on advocating for the rights and experiences of women. 
Some criticisms focus on the dynamics of power and privilege that men may bring to feminist spaces. Concerns have been raised that men, even when acting with good intentions, might unintentionally replicate patriarchal patterns by dominating discussions, taking leadership roles, or centering their own experiences. This can sometimes overshadow the voices and experiences of women, which are at the core of feminist movements. 
Another area of criticism relates to the motivations behind men's involvement. Some feminist perspectives have questioned whether men's support is always rooted in a genuine commitment to dismantling systemic patriarchy or if it might be influenced by self-interest, a desire for social approval, or a focus on issues that primarily benefit men or the women in their personal lives. This leads to discussions about the importance of authentic allyship that prioritizes the needs and goals of women within the movement. 
Furthermore, some critiques from separatist feminist viewpoints argue that men, due to their lived experiences within a patriarchal system, cannot fully understand the oppression faced by women and therefore cannot truly be feminists or lead feminist efforts. This perspective emphasizes the unique experiences of women as fundamental to feminist consciousness and action. 
It is also important to note the historical context of some male involvement. For example, in the suffragist movement, some male supporters were criticized for advocating only for the voting rights of white women, demonstrating how biases can intersect with and limit the scope of allyship. 
In contemporary contexts, criticisms have also emerged regarding online spaces and the potential for anti-feminist individuals or groups to co-opt feminist language or spaces for misogynistic purposes. This highlights the ongoing challenges of distinguishing genuine support from performative or harmful behavior within mixed-gender online discussions about feminism. 
These criticisms are part of ongoing discussions within feminist thought about effective allyship and the complexities of power dynamics in social movements. They do not represent a monolithic view and are debated among different feminist perspectives.

how about ancient examples of men supporting womens rights, BC
Finding ancient examples of men supporting women's rights during the BC era is challenging due to limited and often male-centric historical records. However, scholars have uncovered instances of male support for women's autonomy and rights, particularly in legal and philosophical realms. 
Ancient Egypt (pre-30 BC)
In Ancient Egypt, women enjoyed a high degree of legal and economic independence compared to many other ancient cultures. Men, including pharaohs, were involved in maintaining this system. 
Property rights: Egyptian women could own and inherit property, a right enshrined in the legal system supported by male rulers.
Legal equality: Men and women were considered equal in the eyes of the law and could serve as witnesses in court.
Pharaohs' decrees: Male pharaohs issued decrees and upheld legal codes that reinforced these rights, ensuring that women could conduct business and manage their own assets. 
Greek philosophers (5th–4th centuries BC)
While many Greek writers were misogynistic, some male philosophers expressed more egalitarian views, albeit with conditions.
Plato: In his work Republic, Plato controversially argued that in an ideal society, women of the "guardian class" could receive the same education and training as men, and could even serve as soldiers or rulers. While still viewing women as physically weaker, his willingness to consider their potential in public life was radical for its time.
Plutarch: This Greek philosopher, who wrote later but drew on earlier history, documented numerous examples of virtuous and brave women in his writings. He acknowledged the agency and rational decision-making abilities of women. 
Spartan society (pre-371 BC)
The highly militarized society of Sparta had a more progressive attitude toward women than other Greek city-states, with male figures supporting women's enhanced status.
Aristotle's criticism: The philosopher Aristotle, a fervent misogynist, complained that Spartan women had too much power and that women owned over 40% of Sparta's land. His outrage confirms that some Spartan men supported women's ability to own and control property.
Physical fitness: Spartan men encouraged their wives and daughters to engage in physical activities and training to produce strong sons for the military. This emphasis on women's physical well-being was an exception in the ancient world. 
Roman Republic (pre-27 BC)
Despite its patriarchal structure, the Roman Republic saw instances where men either supported women's causes or were forced to yield to them.
Repeal of the Lex Oppia (195 BC): When Roman matrons protested a law restricting their use of luxury goods, they were met by male politicians, including the misogynistic Cato the Elder, who opposed the repeal. However, the women's sustained protest forced the hand of other male legislators, who ultimately repealed the law.
Changing marriage laws: As the Republic drew to a close, some Roman men supported legal changes that gave women greater freedom from male guardianship (manus). This allowed women more control over their property and legal affairs. 
Legal codification in Mesopotamia (pre-539 BC)
Ancient Near Eastern legal codes, though ultimately restrictive, reveal men's involvement in setting legal standards that also granted women some protections.
Code of Hammurabi (c. 1754 BC): This code, issued by the Babylonian king, recognized the right for women to own property and forbade arbitrary ill-treatment by their husbands. While the code reinforced patriarchal norms, it also served as a legal check on abusive male authority.
Cylinder seals in Sumeria (c. 2400 BC): The existence of cylinder seals belonging to women suggests they were legally permitted to sign contracts and engage in business. Male scribes and administrators upheld this system, as they were the ones who recorded and enforced the laws.
