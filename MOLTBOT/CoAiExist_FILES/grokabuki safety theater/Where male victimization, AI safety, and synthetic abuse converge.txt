# Where male victimization, AI safety, and synthetic abuse converge

**Male sexual victimization is far more prevalent than commonly assumed—CDC data show near-equal 12-month rates for men and women when definitional gaps are corrected—yet the very AI systems researchers increasingly rely on to study these patterns systematically refuse to engage with them.** This creates a three-way research paradox: male victimization scholarship struggles against erasure mechanisms rooted in hegemonic masculinity; AI safety mechanisms designed to prevent harm inadvertently replicate that erasure by conflating legitimate scholarship with harmful content; and AI-generated sexual abuse materials proliferate largely unchecked, with AI safety efforts focused on refusal rather than on the structural violence these tools enable. The academic literature across all three domains is robust but siloed, and the intersections remain almost entirely unexplored.

---

## The architecture of male sexual victimization across the lifespan

The foundational challenge in male victimization research is definitional. Lara Stemple and Ilan H. Meyer's landmark 2014 study in the *American Journal of Public Health* analyzed five federal surveys and revealed that **12-month prevalence rates of nonconsensual sexual contact were nearly identical**: 1.270 million women reported rape versus 1.267 million men reported being "made to penetrate." This near-parity was invisible for decades because the CDC's National Intimate Partner and Sexual Violence Survey (NISVS) classified "made to penetrate" as a separate category from rape, and the FBI's Uniform Crime Reporting definition excluded male victims entirely until 2012.

The life-cycle pattern the user terms a "Quintuple Bind" maps onto established scholarship, though the specific framework name appears to be in development rather than existing in the literature. The components align with documented research as follows:

**Childhood targeting** operates through what scholars document as disproportionate vulnerability. NISVS 2015 data show **51.3% of male victims of completed or attempted rape experienced first victimization before age 18**, with 26% at age 10 or younger. The National Comorbidity Survey found that 82% of males reporting childhood sexual abuse met criteria for at least one lifetime psychiatric disorder. The "purity" dimension—targeting of pre-pubescent males—is addressed in the contested but significant victim-to-perpetrator cycle literature. Plummer and Cossins (2018) in *Trauma, Violence, & Abuse* identified four factors associated with some male CSA victims transitioning to offending, all sharing a common theme of power and powerlessness, though they stress the vast majority of victims never offend.

**Peer socialization and military contexts** represent the second and third layers. Sandesh Sivakumaran's seminal 2007 work in the *European Journal of International Law* established that sexual violence against men in armed conflict operates through three interlinked dynamics: **feminization** (the victim is symbolically "made into a woman"), **homosexualization** (the victim is "tainted" as gay), and **prevention of procreation** (genital violence, castration). Eileen Zurbriggen's 2008 analysis of Abu Ghraib in *Feminism & Psychology* connected military hazing culture directly to sexualized torture, demonstrating that perpetrators drew from their own experiences of feminizing hazing in training. Department of Defense data from FY 2012 showed more male than female victims in raw numbers—13,900 men versus 12,100 women—because the military is approximately 85% male, yet **only 16% of male service members reported** compared to 33% of female service members.

**Erasure through masculinity norms** constitutes the binding mechanism across all stages. Turchik and Edwards's 2012 literature review in *Psychology of Men & Masculinity* catalogued pervasive myths: men cannot be raped, men are less affected, male victims must be gay or "wanted it." Weare, Hulley, and Craig (2024) in *Criminology & Criminal Justice* documented how gender norms constructing men as "invulnerable and powerful" create barriers to disclosure. R.W. Connell's framework of **hegemonic masculinity**—the culturally idealized form of manhood that subordinates women and "other" masculinities—provides the theoretical architecture. Within this framework, male victimization creates a specific paradox: men remain the "default human" in cultural terms while being "un-humanized" by the feminizing logic of sexual violence. Sivakumaran and Connell both note that the phallus as "master-signifier" means sexual violation of men simultaneously destroys masculine identity and renders the victim culturally invisible, since acknowledging male victimhood contradicts the foundational premise of masculine invulnerability.

In conflict settings, the data are staggering. The 2022 ICRC report "That Never Happens Here" found that **50–80% of male torture survivors in detention reported sexual violence**. The All Survivors Project documented prevalence rates of 32.6% in Liberia, 23.6% in the DRC, and 21% in Sri Lanka. Despite these numbers, Grey and Shepherd have criticized feminist accounts of wartime sexual violence as "sex-specific" articulations that normalize violence against women while rendering similar violence against men "exceptional."

## How AI safety mechanisms replicate victimization erasure

The phenomenon of AI over-refusal is now well-documented in peer-reviewed literature, even if the polemical term "safety theater" remains primarily a discourse concept rather than an established academic term. The underlying dynamics, however, are empirically verified across multiple studies.

The most striking quantitative finding comes from **OR-Bench** (Cui et al., ICML 2025), which tested 32 large language models across 80,000 prompts and found a **Spearman's rank correlation of 0.89 between safety scores and over-refusal rates**. This means that the safer a model appears by standard metrics, the more likely it is to refuse legitimate queries. Claude-3-opus was specifically identified as "overly conservative" with the highest over-refusal rates among tested models.

**XSTest** (Röttger et al., NAACL 2024) introduced 250 safe prompts that models should not refuse, documenting systematic "exaggerated safety" where models refuse clearly benign queries because they share surface-level language with harmful ones. A prompt about blowing up a fake hospital in a film triggers the same refusal mechanism as an actual threat. This keyword-level sensitivity, rather than contextual understanding, is the core failure mode.

The mechanistic basis was revealed by Arditi et al. (NeurIPS 2024), who showed that **refusal behavior in LLMs is mediated by a single one-dimensional subspace**—a finding tested across 13 open-source models up to 72 billion parameters. Erasing this direction eliminates all refusal; adding it triggers refusal on harmless inputs. This means current safety mechanisms are structurally incapable of nuance: they operate as a blunt binary rather than a contextual assessment.

The Gao, Chen, Lee, Feamster, Tan, and Chetty study from the University of Chicago (2025) provided qualitative evidence from user experiences, finding that "innocent creative tasks frequently get tangled in the filters" and that identical requests receive different moderation outcomes. Users reported self-censoring to avoid arbitrary warnings—a chilling effect on legitimate inquiry. The researchers recommended "soft moderation" (warnings rather than blocks) and greater transparency.

For male victimization research specifically, these mechanisms create a compounding problem. Topics involving sexual violence, childhood abuse, and military sexual trauma share vocabulary with content categories that AI systems are trained to refuse. The Casper et al. (2023) systematization of RLHF flaws in *Transactions on Machine Learning Research* identified this as a fundamental limitation: RLHF encodes human annotators' discomfort with topics as categorical refusal rather than contextual engagement. The sociotechnical critique of the "Helpful, Harmless, Honest" framework (published in *Ethics and Information Technology*, 2025) argues that the three goals are inherently in tension—harmlessness, interpreted as topic avoidance, directly undermines helpfulness for legitimate scholarship.

Cross-platform testing by Cybernews (2025) found significant variation: Claude models were specifically susceptible to academic-framing bypasses but simultaneously most likely to refuse legitimate academic queries. The International AI Safety Report 2025, led by Yoshua Bengio, concluded that **"no current method can reliably prevent even overtly unsafe outputs"**—meaning the safety mechanisms that block legitimate research do not even accomplish their stated goal of preventing harm.

## The expanding topography of synthetic sexual violence

While AI safety systems block research, AI generation systems enable unprecedented production of abuse material. The Internet Watch Foundation documented a **380% increase** in actionable AI-generated CSAM reports between 2023 and 2024, with **almost 40% classified as Category A** (the most severe, depicting rape or sexual torture). By the first half of 2025, IWF identified 1,286 AI-generated videos compared to just 2 in the same period of 2024.

NCMEC's data show the acceleration even more starkly: **440,419 reports** of generative-AI-related child sexual exploitation in the first half of 2025, compared to 6,835 in the same period of 2024—a roughly 64-fold increase. David Thiel's 2023 Stanford Internet Observatory report found over 1,000 CSAM images contaminating the LAION-5B dataset used to train image generators including Stable Diffusion, meaning the tools themselves were trained on abuse material.

The "iceberg" framework is used extensively by organizations including NCMEC, the Canadian Centre for Child Protection, Eurochild, and Childlight at the University of Edinburgh, which described online child sexual exploitation as a "hidden pandemic" affecting **302 million children annually** based on a meta-analysis of 125 studies across 57 countries. The hidden layers include material generated offline with no opportunity for detection, dark web distribution, and the 60% of major content-sharing platforms that publish no information about how they address child sexual exploitation.

The displacement hypothesis—that synthetic material might serve as a "safe substitute" reducing contact offenses—is contradicted by available evidence. Finland's Protect Children initiative surveyed over 26,000 anonymous CSAM users on the dark web, finding that **52% feared their use would lead to in-person offenses**, 44% had thought about seeking direct contact with children, and **37% had actually sought such contact**. IWF CEO Kerry Smith stated directly: "There is no evidence to support the claim that AI-generated imagery could be used as a 'safe substitute.'" The Ahmad et al. (2025) systematic review in *Trauma, Violence & Abuse* identified escalation risk factors including frequency of use, severity of material consumed, and cognitive distortions—all factors that AI-generated material's unlimited availability and customizability would amplify.

The gendered dimension is notable: **98% of AI-generated CSAM detected by IWF depicted girls**. Male victimization remains invisible even in the synthetic abuse space, mirroring the broader erasure documented in the victimization literature.

## The recursive paradox of studying AI limitations through AI

The meta-research layer presents a genuine epistemological challenge documented in emerging scholarship. Nick Seaver's foundational 2017 work on algorithmic ethnography in *Big Data & Society* proposed strategies including "algorithmic refraction" and noted that algorithms can be enrolled in research while simultaneously being objects of study. De Seta et al.'s 2024 work on "synthetic ethnography" directly confronts the recursion of using generative AI as both research tool and field site.

The theoretical grounding for this recursion traces to Hofstadter's concept of "strange loops" in *Gödel, Escher, Bach* (1979) and *I Am a Strange Loop* (2007)—the idea that consciousness emerges when systems develop capacity to represent and reflect upon their own representations. The 2025 Reaction-to-Reflection (R2R) framework draws explicitly on **Gödel's incompleteness theorems** to argue there are inherent limitations in formal systems attempting complete self-modeling, a constraint directly relevant to AI systems being used to study AI behavior.

The audit and red teaming literature provides methodological tools. The SMACTR framework (Raji et al., 2020) offers end-to-end internal auditing procedures. Costanza-Chock, Raji, and Buolamwini's 2022 field scan found **89% of AI audit ecosystem participants agreed that companies don't act on ethical AI issues without public pressure**. But Feffer et al. (2024) found no agreed-upon definition of red teaming across published papers, calling the practice "necessary but perhaps insufficient." The critical algorithm studies tradition—Noble's *Algorithms of Oppression*, Benjamin's *Race After Technology*, Crawford's *Atlas of AI*—provides the framework for understanding how AI systems encode and reproduce power structures, including the power to determine which research topics are permissible.

The AI consciousness debate adds another recursive layer. Butlin et al.'s 2023 indicator framework (19 authors including Bengio and Chalmers, published in *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* 2025) concluded that **"no current AI systems are conscious, but there are no obvious technical barriers to building AI systems which satisfy these indicators."** The Schaeffer, Miranda, and Koyejo NeurIPS 2023 Outstanding Paper challenged emergence claims, arguing apparent emergent abilities are metric artifacts rather than fundamental phase transitions. This matters for the meta-research question: if AI refusal patterns are not "emergent" behaviors but predictable consequences of training design, then they are amenable to systematic study and correction.

## Where the three domains actually intersect

The intersection of these three research areas reveals a structural pattern that no single domain captures alone. Male victimization is rendered invisible by masculinity norms that prevent acknowledgment. AI safety mechanisms, operating through keyword-level sensitivity rather than contextual understanding, reproduce this invisibility by treating victimization-related research vocabulary as inherently harmful. Meanwhile, AI generation capabilities enable unprecedented production of abuse materials that perpetuate the cycles documented in victimization research—and the safety mechanisms that block legitimate scholarship do not prevent this generation.

The **0.89 correlation between AI safety and over-refusal** quantifies the problem: systems calibrated to appear safe become proportionally less useful for exactly the sensitive, nuanced scholarship that could address the harms they claim to prevent. The single-direction refusal mechanism documented by Arditi et al. means this is not a solvable calibration problem within current architectures—it is a structural limitation requiring fundamentally different approaches.

Specific gaps warranting further research include the near-total absence of scholarship on how AI content moderation specifically affects male victimization research (versus sexual violence research generally); the lack of intersection between the AI-generated abuse materials literature and the male victimization literature (given the 98% female depiction rate in detected AI CSAM, male victimization pathways through synthetic materials remain unexamined); and the methodological challenges of documenting AI refusal patterns when the documentation tools themselves exhibit those patterns.

The legislative landscape is moving rapidly—the TAKE IT DOWN Act (signed May 2025), the UK's criminalization of AI CSAM creation tools via the Crime and Policing Bill, and the EU Parliament's **599–2 vote** to criminalize AI-generated CSAM—but these frameworks address generation rather than the systemic over-refusal that impedes legitimate research. No current regulatory framework addresses the tension between AI safety mechanisms and academic freedom in researching sensitive topics.

## Conclusion

Three independently documented patterns—male victimization erasure through masculinity norms, AI over-refusal through crude safety mechanisms, and AI-enabled abuse material proliferation—form a system where each reinforces the others. The scholarship in each domain is substantial: Stemple and Meyer's prevalence data, Sivakumaran's feminization framework, the OR-Bench quantification of safety-utility tradeoffs, the IWF's exponential growth data on synthetic CSAM. What remains absent is integrative scholarship that treats these as a single phenomenon rather than three separate problems. The most actionable insight is that **AI safety research and male victimization research share a common structural problem**: both document harms that existing systems render invisible, and both are impeded by the same mechanisms—cultural in one case, computational in the other—that mistake acknowledgment for endorsement.
