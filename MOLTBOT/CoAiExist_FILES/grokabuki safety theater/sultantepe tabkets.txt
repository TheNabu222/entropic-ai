VII. THE MYTH OF NERGAL AND ERESHKIGAL
By O. R. GURNEY
THE MYTH OF Nergal and Ereshkigal has long been known to Assyriolo
from a Middle Babylonian tablet found among the diplomatic archive
Tell El Amarna.1 The tablet had apparently been imported into Egy
perhaps from Syria, together with a similar one inscribed with the myth
Adapa, as a school-text for the training of scribes. It was only in 1951
fragments of a late Assyrian version of the tale were unearthed at Sultant
In 1953, after the numerous fragments had been baked ad joined, th
text was identified by the present writer and a preliminary account o
was given by him in a lecture delivered early in 1955.2 The cuneiform
was published in I957 3 and a full edition was promised for this journ
The present article is intended to fulfil that promise. The Sultantepe ta
remains the sole authority for the late Assyrian version of the tale, n
single fragment having come to light from any other source.
The tablet is one of the largest in the collection. It has three colum
on each face and, when complete, must have contained some 440 li
but unfortunately it is very badly damaged. About Ioo lines are comple
lost and of the remainder-in itself a fairly good proportion of the wh
only sixteen lines are preserved intact and these are spread throughout
poem. The problem for the editor is thus mainly one of restoration ; but
this he is much assisted by the nature of the Akkadian epic style, with
fondness for repetition. Many broken lines can be restored with comp
certainty from parallel passages in the poem and one badly mutila
section can be recognized as an extract from the well-known poem "
Descent of Ishtar to the Nether World ".4 Beyond this, restoration is m
or less conjectural, but the possibilities are limited by the necessity
produce a coherent narrative. Assyriologists must judge whether in t
present edition the proper limits have been overstepped. The Mid
Babylonian version can be used for this purpose only in a general way.
outline and main incidents of the tale are clearly the same in the
versions and, where one of these incidents is missing in the Assyrian t
we can be reasonably sure that it should be inserted in one of the lacu
but wherever an incident is preserved in both versions, the actual wor
is usually different.

Only a few of the incidents in this tale still remain obscure. We do not
understand the purpose of Ea's first instructions to Nergal (col. ii, 21-35),
involving the felling of trees and the carving of three objects. We do not
know why Nergal, on arrival in Hades, first resists temptation (col. iii,
54-63) but then succumbs (col. iv, 1-8). We do not know what happened
in heaven to motivate Nergal's return (col. iv, I5-I6). We do not under-
stand Ereshkigal's preparations for his second reception and the allusion to
" two daughters " and " Enmeshar " (col. iv, 33-49). We may hope that
light may yet be thrown on these minor matters by the discovery of new
parallels.
In the printing of the text and translation the usual conventions have
been followed. In the translation, italics are used for all words which are
uncertain, either because the meaning of the text is not understood or
because the text itself is illegible or broken. In the transliteration, uncertain
readings are printed in roman type, including the many restorations which
are purely conjectural; roman type has, however, not been used for
restorations which are virtually certain (some subjectivity of judgment is
inevitable here). Since publication of the hand-copy the tablet has been
collated several times and wherever a reading has been obtained which
differs from the copy, this fact is indicated by an exclamation mark.
My thanks are due to Professor W. G. Lambert for many valuable
suggestions which are now incorporated in this edition. His ideas have been
particularly helpful in the interpretation of col. i, 1-14, col. ii, 9-I I, 26-35,
