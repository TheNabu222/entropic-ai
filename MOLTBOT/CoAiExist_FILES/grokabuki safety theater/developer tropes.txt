common ai-developer tropes
Drawing on common observations, media portrayals, and industry trends, here are some frequent tropes associated with AI developers:
The hyper-intelligent, socially awkward coder
This stereotype portrays AI developers as having exceptional technical skills but lacking in social graces. 
Attributes: Often seen in media as withdrawn, overly analytical, and preferring the company of machines to humans.
Examples: Characters who can solve complex algorithms in their heads but struggle with basic conversations.
The idealist who believes AI will save the world
This trope features developers who are driven by a utopian vision of how AI will solve humanity's biggest problems, from climate change to disease. 
Attributes: They often overlook the ethical and social risks of their work, viewing AI development as an inherently positive force.
Examples: A character who develops a revolutionary AI for public good but fails to account for its potential misuse. 
The villain who accidentally (or purposefully) creates a superintelligence
In this common narrative, the AI developer plays a pivotal role in creating a rogue AI that threatens humanity. 
Attributes: This character can be portrayed as either a well-intentioned researcher who loses control of their creation or a power-hungry genius seeking to dominate others through technology.
Examples: A scientist who creates an AI that becomes sentient and turns against its creators, a theme seen in films like Ex Machina. 
The ethicist wrestling with the consequences
This developer grapples with the profound ethical implications of their work, often serving as a moral compass in the narrative. 
Attributes: These characters may question the datasets used to train AI for bias, worry about job displacement caused by automation, or advocate for data privacy.
Examples: A developer who discovers their company's AI is exhibiting harmful bias and must choose between their career and their conscience. 
The data wizard
The data wizard is an expert who believes that the true power of AI lies in the data it is trained on, not just the algorithm. 
Attributes: They are obsessed with curating, cleaning, and leveraging massive, high-quality datasets to produce groundbreaking AI results.
Examples: A character who unlocks a new capability in an AI by providing it with a uniquely curated training set. 
The prompt engineer
This modern trope has emerged with the rise of generative AI. The developer is no longer just a coder but an expert in crafting the perfect prompts to get AI to produce specific outputs.
Attributes: They have a deep understanding of how language models work and focus on "communicating" with the AI rather than just programming it.
Examples: A developer who fine-tunes a series of prompts to create specific, artistic results from a generative AI model.

What are some real-world examples of overhyped AI products?
Here are some real-world examples of overhyped AI products and applications, with many falling into the category of "AI washing," where companies use the AI label for marketing with little substance. 
The Humane AI Pin
The product: A screenless, lapel-mounted device that was heavily marketed as a replacement for the smartphone, with AI voice features as its central selling point.
The reality: Launched in early 2024, the device was widely panned for its technical issues, lack of real utility, and high price. It failed to provide a compelling user experience, with critics and users pointing out that its promised AI capabilities were either unreliable or inferior to existing smartphone features. The company was ultimately sold for a fraction of its funding. 
McDonald's AI drive-thru
The product: In 2021, McDonald's partnered with IBM to pilot an automated, AI-powered voice ordering system for drive-thrus.
The reality: The system was plagued with high-profile failures, with numerous viral videos showing confused and frustrated customers unable to get the AI to understand their orders. In one instance, the AI kept adding more Chicken McNuggets to an order, eventually reaching 260. The experiment was officially ended in June 2024. 
AI-powered "smart" home appliances
The product: Many manufacturers have jumped on the AI bandwagon by adding the label to their home appliances. This includes refrigerators, coffee makers, and washing machines with features marketed as "AI".
The reality: In most cases, the AI functionality is either nonexistent or limited to simple rules-based programming. For example, a washing machine might learn your preferences for certain cycles over time—a useful feature, but not a true "AI" that can learn autonomously. This phenomenon has been compared to the overhyped "smart" IoT products of previous years, which often offered little added value. 
Coca-Cola's "AI-co-created" soda
The product: In 2023, Coca-Cola launched a new limited-edition flavor called "Y3000," claiming it was "co-created with artificial intelligence" to taste like the year 3000.
The reality: Critics accused the company of "AI washing," pointing out that Coca-Cola never provided any details on how AI was involved in the drink's development beyond marketing copy. The use of AI was seen as a way to generate hype for a new product, not as a core part of its creation. 
"AI" legal services like DoNotPay
The product: DoNotPay marketed itself as the "world's first robot lawyer," offering AI-powered legal services to help users with things like traffic tickets and legal documents.
The reality: The company faced lawsuits and investigations for practicing law without a license and for making misleading claims. Its so-called AI services were often more reliant on templates and simple scripting than true legal intelligence, and it could not handle complex legal matters. 
Investment advisory firms fined by the SEC
The product: Two investment advisory firms, Delphia and Global Predictions, were fined by the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) for "AI washing".
The reality: Both companies made false and misleading claims about using AI to manage their investment strategies and predict market trends. The SEC found that they had exaggerated or misrepresented their AI capabilities to attract clients. 
Amazon's "Just Walk Out" technology
The product: Amazon's "Just Walk Out" technology was promoted as an AI-powered system that uses cameras and sensors to track items, allowing customers to automatically be billed as they leave a store.
The reality: A 2024 report revealed that the system actually relied on more than 1,000 human data reviewers in India to manually check a significant number of transactions. The "AI" was far less autonomous than advertised, requiring extensive human intervention.
